Mainstack — Bookings

Product design · UX writing · PRD & handover documentation — built from scratch

+

The structure below reflects the type of thinking and documentation I produced for this project — it is a generalized illustration of my process, not a reproduction of the original PRD or internal materials.

My role

I owned Bookings end to end as a 0-to-1 product: I designed the flow and screens, wrote all product copy, authored the PRD, and produced the handover documentation engineering used to build and QA the feature. There was no existing scheduling product to redesign — every decision, from information architecture to edge-case handling, started with me.

The problem

Creators had no way to sell time — consultations, sessions, calls — without juggling a separate calendar tool and manually collecting payment outside the platform. The product needed to unify availability, booking, and payment into a single flow that worked reliably across time zones with no manual reconciliation.

PRD
  1. Purpose & background — Creators were losing revenue and time to manual scheduling workflows stitched together across calendar apps, invoicing tools, and DMs. Bookings consolidates availability management, checkout, and confirmation into one native flow.
  2. Scope — In scope (v1): single-creator availability, one-on-one session booking, combined booking + payment, automated confirmation and reminder notifications, cancellation/rescheduling with policy enforcement. Out of scope (v1): group/cohort bookings, recurring session packages, third-party calendar sync (flagged as a v2 dependency).
  3. User personas — The Creator, who sells 1:1 time and needs control over availability and protection against no-shows; the Client, booking a session for the first time, who needs certainty that slot, timezone, and payment are all correctly locked in.
  4. User flows — Two primary flows: the creator sets availability and publishes bookable slots; the client selects a slot, pays, receives confirmation, and can cancel or reschedule. Each flow was documented as a step-by-step diagram with corresponding screen states and copy annotations for engineering handoff.
  5. Functional requirements — Slot availability calendar with creator-defined windows; automatic timezone conversion at display time; combined booking-and-payment transaction; system-generated confirmation and reminder notifications; cancellation/rescheduling flow with configurable creator policy; real-time creator notification on booking/cancellation events.
  6. Acceptance criteria — Defined per requirement, e.g. for timezone conversion: given a client in a different timezone than the creator, when they view a slot, the displayed time reflects the client's local timezone with the creator's timezone shown as reference.
  7. Edge cases & error states — Double-booking race conditions, cancellation after payment capture, daylight saving time transitions shifting a confirmed slot, payment failure after a slot is tentatively held.
  8. Dependencies & stakeholders — Cross-functional sign-off from engineering (booking logic, payment integration), design (visual system), and support (cancellation/refund policy language).
Handover documentation

Produced the handoff package engineering used to build against: annotated flow diagrams, a full content/copy deck mapped to each screen state (including error and empty states), and a QA checklist derived directly from the acceptance criteria — so QA could test against the same source of truth as the PRD, rather than reverse-engineering expected behavior from screens alone.

Mainstack — Hosted Courses

Product design · UX writing · PRD & handover documentation — built from scratch

+

As with Bookings, the structure below is a generalized illustration of my process — not a reproduction of the original PRD or internal materials.

My role

Like Bookings, Hosted Courses was a 0-to-1 build: I designed the flow, wrote all product copy, authored the PRD, and produced the handover documentation for engineering. This meant defining the full creator-to-student experience from a blank slate — course structuring, pricing, payment, progress tracking, and completion.

The problem

Creators were stitching together multiple separate tools — a payment processor, a video host, a certificate generator — just to run one paid course. Hosted Courses needed to consolidate that into one coherent product, simple on both the creator side and the student side, across a global, multi-currency audience.

PRD
  1. Purpose & background — Creators were losing time, revenue, and audience reach to fragmented tooling. Hosted Courses is positioned as a native, all-in-one alternative that removes the technical setup barrier to launching a paid course.
  2. Scope — In scope (v1): module/lesson-based course structure, multi-currency pricing at checkout, student progress tracking, certificate generation on completion. Out of scope (v1): live cohort scheduling, multi-instructor courses (flagged as v2 candidates).
  3. User personas — The Creator, an expert packaging knowledge into a structured, sellable course; the Student, enrolling from anywhere globally, needing clarity on cost in their own currency, visibility into progress, and proof of completion.
  4. User flows — The creator builds a course (creates modules, sets pricing, publishes); the student enrolls, pays, progresses through modules, and receives a certificate. Each flow was mapped step-by-step with corresponding screen states and content annotations for handoff.
  5. Functional requirements — Module/lesson structuring with drag-and-drop ordering; multi-currency pricing displayed in the student's local currency at checkout; a student-facing progress dashboard; certificate template and auto-generation logic triggered on completion; creator-facing analytics on enrollment and completion rates.
  6. Acceptance criteria — Defined per requirement, e.g. for currency display: given a student outside the creator's home currency, when they view the course price, the price is shown converted to their local currency alongside the base price.
  7. Edge cases & error states — Refund requests mid-course and their effect on certificate eligibility; partial completion not qualifying for a certificate; currency conversion mismatches between viewing and checkout; a module edited or removed after students have enrolled.
  8. Dependencies & stakeholders — Coordinated with engineering (course infrastructure, payment integration), design (certificate templates), and support (refund/partial-completion policy language).
Handover documentation

Delivered a handoff package parallel to the Bookings documentation: annotated flow diagrams for both the creator and student journeys, a full content deck mapped to every screen and state, and a QA checklist built directly from the acceptance criteria so testing could be verified against the same source of truth used to build the feature.

Mainstack — Link-in-Bio

Redesign · template system · UX writing

+

Generalized to respect confidentiality — describes the type of redesign and approach, not internal materials.

My role

I redesigned Link-in-Bio around a template system. Instead of every creator starting from a blank canvas, I designed a set of ready-made templates mapped to distinct creator personas — for example, a coach selling sessions, a course creator, or a creator selling a mix of products — so a new user could self-select the layout closest to their business model and adapt it rather than build a page from zero. I wrote the copy for the templates themselves and for the persona-selection flow that routes a creator to the right starting point.

The problem

Link-in-Bio is a creator's single landing page — simultaneously their storefront, their identity, and often their only website. A blank-page starting point was a drop-off point in onboarding: creators didn't have a reference point for what "good" looked like for their specific business, so setup stalled before a page ever went live.

Approach

I ran an informal audit of existing creator pages to identify recurring business-model patterns, then consolidated those into a small set of named personas rather than generic "layout 1 / layout 2" options. Each persona template came with its own default copy, structured so it read as a helpful, editable starting point rather than boilerplate a creator had to delete before the page felt like theirs. The persona-selection flow itself was written to route a creator to a match using plain, business-focused language rather than abstract design terminology.

Content principles applied
  • Persona-first content mapping — copy tailored to the business model, not the layout
  • Progressive disclosure in the setup flow — ask one question, not a form
  • Voice neutrality — template copy brand-agnostic enough to suit a wide range of creators while still feeling specific rather than generic

Mainstack — Pay-in-Tranches

UX writing — copy only (design was already in place)

+

Generalized to respect confidentiality — describes the type of writing problem and approach, not internal materials.

My role

Pay-in-Tranches already existed as a shipped feature when I joined this work — my scope was copy, not design or product definition. The feature let creators split a customer's payment into 2, 3, or 4 installments, but the underlying concept was written in financial/technical language that didn't translate to a non-financial, often first-time buyer.

The problem

A customer isn't completing one transaction with Pay-in-Tranches, they're entering an ongoing payment commitment — so ambiguous copy directly threatens checkout completion and trust. The existing language leaned on internal terminology without translating it into what a buyer was actually agreeing to.

Content strategy

Rather than a screen-by-screen rewrite in isolation, I approached this as a microcopy audit: I inventoried every touchpoint where a customer encountered payment-plan language and rewrote them against a single content principle — always state the concrete number, amount, and date, never the abstract mechanism. I also wrote a short internal style note for the pattern so future copy on the feature stayed consistent.

Blood Donation App

Product design

+
The problem

Blood donation apps solve a matching problem under pressure: connecting an urgent need — a hospital or patient requiring a specific blood type, often on a tight timeline — with a nearby, eligible, willing donor. Most of these apps fail in the same two places: the emergency request flow reads as alarming rather than clear, and the donor eligibility questions are written in clinical language that confuses people who'd otherwise be happy to donate.

What I focused on

Onboarding and eligibility copy, and the emergency request flow — writing for two very different emotional states in the same product: a donor calmly signing up, and a requester in genuine crisis.

Design/writing decisions
  • Kept emergency request screens short and directive rather than clinical, so urgency reads as clarity, not panic
  • Rewrote eligibility questions in plain language, turning clinical exclusion criteria into plain yes/no questions
  • Built trust into steps that ask for sensitive health/location data by explaining, in one line, why each piece of information was needed before asking for it

Tour Guide Website

Content design

+
The problem

Travel discovery sites have to do two contradictory things in the same sentence: make a place feel worth visiting and make it easy to actually plan or book. Sites that lean too far into inspiration become beautiful but unusable; sites that lean too far into usability read like a spreadsheet.

What I focused on

Content structure from destination to experience to booking, and the microcopy connecting each step — making sure every "explore" moment had a clear, low-pressure next action.

Design/writing decisions
  • Structured content hierarchy so a visitor could move from browsing destinations to booking a specific experience without hitting a dead end
  • Wrote CTAs that invited rather than pushed, to match the browsing mindset of someone still deciding
  • Kept destination copy sensory and specific rather than generic

Instructional Writing

Canvas / Syllabus Design
A syllabus is documentation. It's the first onboarding artifact a student ever touches for a course — and like any good onboarding doc, it has to work for a total stranger with zero context, no one to ask, and real consequences if they misunderstand it.

As an Instructor of Record at the University of Florida, I design and maintain the Canvas course infrastructure for ENC 1136 (Multimodal Writing and Digital Literacy) and previously ENC 1102 (Argument and Persuasion) — syllabi, assignment instructions, grading rubrics, and module sequencing that students rely on with no other point of contact. I treat it exactly like product onboarding: what does a first-time user need to know before they can act, what's the minimum information at each step, and where do people get stuck if I don't pre-empt it in the copy itself.

The skills are identical — audience analysis, information hierarchy, anticipating confusion before it happens, revising based on real user behavior. The only difference is the interface.
